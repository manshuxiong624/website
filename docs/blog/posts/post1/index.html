What Is a p-Value? A Coin, a Surprise, and a Common Mistake

statistics
data-science
Published

September 14, 2026

You flip a coin 20 times and get 15 heads. That seems suspicious: a fair coin should produce heads about half the time. But fair coins do not alternate perfectly between heads and tails. Random variation can produce an uneven result.

So how much evidence does this outcome provide against the claim that the coin is fair?

A p-value helps us answer this question by asking how unusual the result would be under a particular assumption. Understanding that starting assumption is the key to interpreting the number correctly.

Start by assuming the coin is fair

Our starting claim, called the null hypothesis, is that the probability of heads is 0.5. We also assume that the flips are independent, and we decide in advance to flip exactly 20 times.

Before collecting the data, we choose a two-sided test: we will look for evidence that the coin favors either heads or tails.

Under these assumptions, we expect about 10 heads. Getting 15 heads is five away from that expectation. An equally extreme outcome in the opposite direction would be getting only 5 heads.

The p-value therefore includes the probability of getting 15 or more heads, or 5 or fewer heads, assuming the coin is fair.

Let \(X\) be the number of heads. Under the null hypothesis, \(X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(20, 0.5)\), so

\[ p = P(X \geq 15) + P(X \leq 5) \approx 0.0414. \]

In everyday language: if we repeatedly conducted this experiment with a fair coin, about 4.14% of experiments would produce a result at least this far from 10 heads.

This is a hypothetical example; no external dataset is used.

Why include results “more extreme”?

Why count 16, 17, or even 20 heads when we observed exactly 15?

Because we are evaluating how strongly the result departs from the null hypothesis. If 15 heads makes us suspicious, 20 heads should also count as evidence of a departure.

The probability of one exact outcome does not capture this idea. Indeed, every particular ordered sequence of 20 fair coin flips has the same probability, \(1/2^{20}\). A perfectly ordinary mixed sequence is individually just as unlikely as an all-heads sequence.

Our test instead groups outcomes according to a feature chosen in advance: how far the number of heads is from 10.

The mistake that makes p-values confusing

It is tempting to interpret \(p = 0.0414\) as saying there is only a 4.14% probability that the coin is fair. That reverses the question.

We calculated the probability of certain outcomes assuming fairness. We did not calculate the probability of fairness given the outcomes.

The distinction resembles the difference between “How likely is a wet sidewalk when it rains?” and “How likely is rain when the sidewalk is wet?” Sprinklers can also wet sidewalks. Reversing the question requires additional information.

Similarly, assigning a probability to the coin being fair requires a broader model, including assumptions about possible biases and their prior plausibility. The American Statistical Association explicitly cautions that p-values do not give the probability that a hypothesis is true. (ASA statement)

Does below 0.05 mean we have proved something?

Using a significance threshold of 0.05 chosen in advance, we would reject the fair-coin null hypothesis in this example. However, rejection is a decision under a testing rule, not proof that fairness is impossible.

Nor is 0.05 a magical boundary. Results with p-values of 0.049 and 0.051 provide very similar information, even though they fall on opposite sides of that threshold.

A p-value also does not measure practical importance. In an economics study, a large sample might detect a tiny wage difference that has little economic relevance. A smaller study might fail to detect a meaningful difference because its estimate is imprecise. Effect sizes, uncertainty, and study design therefore belong alongside the p-value. (ASA statement)

The takeaway

A p-value asks: assuming the null hypothesis and the statistical model are correct, how often would we observe a test result this extreme or more extreme?

A small answer flags a mismatch between the observations and those assumptions. It does not tell us the probability that our hypothesis is wrong, prove a discovery, or establish that an effect matters. Its value comes from helping us evaluate evidence while keeping those distinctions clear.
